Someone taps a microphone button in a chat and says a sentence. A moment later, their words appear on screen, rewriting themselves as the sentence goes on, while a ribbon of waves next to them rises and falls with their voice, the way it does in a voice message.

It looks like one feature. It is not. Between the air moving in front of the phone and the text appearing on screen, the sound is measured, sliced, converted, repackaged, counted, compressed into text, shipped over a network and reassembled. At every one of those steps there is a number that has to be exactly right, and a way for it to be silently wrong.

**This is live speech-to-text, not a recording that gets transcribed later.** Nobody records a voice note, saves a file and uploads it for analysis afterwards. The sound is processed while the person is still speaking: it is sliced into short pieces of under a second, each piece is sent the moment it is ready, and the words come back and rewrite themselves while the sentence is still going on. Small temporary audio files do exist inside the pipeline, but they are throwaway slices of a stream, not a finished recording that anyone ever checks or keeps.

This is the story of how we built that pipeline: first inside a chat app, later extracted into a dedicated Swift package. It covers what sound actually is, which Apple APIs we used to reach it, how every byte is treated on its way out, and the bugs that taught us the most. You do not need to know audio. Every section starts in plain words and then goes down to the code.

## First, what is sound?

Sound is air being pushed and pulled. A voice makes the air pressure near your phone rise and fall, hundreds of times per second. The microphone turns that rise and fall into a changing voltage.

Two properties of that wiggle matter for everything that follows:

- **Amplitude** is how far the pressure swings. Big swing, loud sound. A smoothed measure of this is what the live bars show.
- **Frequency** is how fast it swings. Fast swing, high pitch. The pitch of an adult voice sits low, roughly 80 to 300 swings per second (Hz), but its harmonics and the hiss of consonants reach much higher. The old telephone band, 300 to 3,400 Hz, carries enough to follow a call, yet sounds like /s/ and /f/ live between about 4,000 and 8,000 Hz, which is why they blur on a phone line.

A computer cannot store a smooth, continuous wave. It stores **samples**: the height of the wave, measured at fixed instants. Measure often enough and you can redraw the wave faithfully. Measure too rarely and a fast wave gets mistaken for a slower one. Try it:

<SoundWaveLab />

Two things to notice. First, the rule of thumb the lab shows: you can only keep frequencies **below half** the sample rate, and anything above that must be filtered out before sampling or it folds back as a false, lower one. The rule applies to every frequency in the sound, not just the pitch of the voice: a voice pitched at 120 Hz still carries harmonics and hiss far above it. At 16 kHz the limit is 8,000 Hz, high enough to keep those consonants, which is why speech services ask for 16 kHz rather than the 8 kHz of a telephone line, and not the 48 kHz a phone microphone often runs at. Second, the data cost: every sample here is 2 bytes, so one second of 16 kHz mono (one microphone channel, no stereo) is exactly 32,000 bytes.

> **In plain words:** the phone takes tens of thousands of tiny pressure readings every second. Everything in this article is about what we do with that stream of numbers.

## Reaching the microphone: a session, an engine, a tap

iOS does not hand an app the microphone. You negotiate for it in three layers, and our SDK owns each one.

**1. The audio session** says what the app wants to do with audio. We ask for play-and-record with Bluetooth allowed, read the hardware sample rate, and activate the session. This is what the SDK ships:

```swift
try audioSession.setCategory(
    .playAndRecord,
    mode: .spokenAudio,
    options: [.allowBluetooth, .allowBluetoothA2DP]
)
hardwareSampleRate = audioSession.sampleRate
try audioSession.setActive(true)
```

Three things in it are worth changing. `spokenAudio` sounds right but is meant for apps that _play_ speech, like podcasts; for recognition, `measurement` keeps the system's own processing, automatic gain control included, out of the way. `.allowBluetooth` has been renamed `.allowBluetoothHFP` (the hands-free profile, the one that carries a headset microphone), and `.allowBluetoothA2DP` only affects output, so it does nothing for capture. And the sample rate is read before activation, when it can still be stale. The whole block also sits in a `do`/`catch` that only logs, so a session that fails to activate does not stop the recorder from trying to start. The version we would ship:

```swift
try audioSession.setCategory(.playAndRecord, mode: .measurement, options: [.allowBluetoothHFP])
try audioSession.setActive(true)
hardwareSampleRate = audioSession.sampleRate
```

None of these settings give echo cancellation. `AVAudioEngine`'s input has none in any mode unless voice processing is switched on with `setVoiceProcessingEnabled(true)`. Without `.defaultToSpeaker`, anything the app plays goes to the earpiece, which keeps the leak small. Add it, so app sounds come out of the loudspeaker right next to the microphone, and the app has to stay silent while it listens, or turn voice processing on and accept the gain control and noise suppression that come with it, processing that speech services generally prefer to do themselves.

**2. The audio engine** ([`AVAudioEngine`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudioengine)) is a graph of nodes. Audio flows from node to node. Ours is deliberately tiny, because the only node we need is the one that represents the microphone: `inputNode`.

**3. The tap** ([`installTap`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudionode/1387122-installtap)) is a listener bolted onto a node. Every time the node has a batch of fresh audio, the tap closure is called with a buffer. A tap does not change the sound; it lets you read it.

<AudioGraphFlow />

```swift
let format = inputNode.inputFormat(forBus: 0)
inputNode.removeTap(onBus: 0)
inputNode.installTap(onBus: 0, bufferSize: 512, format: format) { buffer, _ in
    // one batch of samples, arriving continuously while we record
}
```

The SDK reads the format from `inputFormat(forBus:)`, the hardware side of the node. On the input node the two sides normally agree, but the tap sits on the output bus, so `outputFormat(forBus:)` is the one that describes what the tap delivers. (As of iOS 27, this closure-only `installTap` is deprecated in favour of a variant that reports errors instead of raising.)

Two defensive details were learned the hard way:

- **Remove before you install.** A node has numbered outputs called buses, and installing a second tap on a bus that already has one raises an exception and crashes ("Only one tap may be installed on any bus"). We always remove first.
- **Do not trust the node until it reports a real format.** Early in a session, or right after an audio route change, the input node can briefly report a format with a sample rate or channel count of zero. Installing a tap or starting the engine in that state crashes the app. So before either we check the format, and if it is not valid we wait a second and look again, up to three attempts:

```swift
// simplified
func waitForValidInputFormat(attempts: Int = 3) async throws -> Bool {
    for attempt in 1...attempts {
        let format = audioEngine.inputNode.inputFormat(forBus: 0)
        if format.sampleRate > 0, format.channelCount > 0 { return true }
        if attempt < attempts { try await Task.sleep(for: .seconds(1)) }
    }
    return false
}
```

> **In plain words:** ask iOS politely for the microphone, build the smallest possible pipe, and refuse to switch it on until the pipe has a real shape.

## Anatomy of one buffer

Each time the tap fires, it hands us an [`AVAudioPCMBuffer`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiopcmbuffer). Inside it is a row of 32-bit floating-point numbers, one per frame for each channel (with one microphone channel, a frame is a single sample), nominally between -1.0 and +1.0. Zero is silence. ±1.0 is full scale; values can briefly exceed it and get clipped when converted to 16-bit. We request a buffer size of 512 frames, but that is only a hint, and it is below the 100 to 400 ms range Apple documents for taps. In practice iOS delivers about 100 ms per callback, roughly 4,800 frames at 48 kHz, which is why the code always reads the real length from the buffer instead of assuming 512.

Everything we do next happens to that row of numbers, in two ways:

1. **Keep it.** The buffer is written into a small temporary WAV file, converted on the way to 16-bit integers, one channel.
2. **Measure it.** The same buffer is reduced to a row of level values for the on-screen bars.

Both are kicked off from the same tap callback. The tap is not called on the real-time render thread, so it could do this work directly; instead the callback hands each buffer to its own small asynchronous task. That is simple and it mostly works, but unstructured tasks carry no ordering guarantee, so two buffers can reach the file out of sequence or race the timer that rotates files. It is the first thing we would revisit (see the end).

## Making loudness visible

The waves that dance while you talk are the part users notice and the part with the most history.

### Version one: one number, on a timer

The first version computed one level value per buffer and stored it. A timer inside the recorder fired every 80 milliseconds and published the latest value to the screen, roughly the length of a single speech sound.

It worked, but it mixed two jobs: the recorder was both measuring the sound and deciding how fast the screen should move. And because the timer and the audio callbacks ran at different paces, a tick could publish the same value twice or skip a buffer entirely.

### Version two: the recorder measures, the view paces

We took the timer out of the recorder. Now each buffer is cut into 100 equal slices, each slice gets its own level value, and the recorder publishes the whole batch of 100 every time a buffer arrives. It no longer has any opinion about the screen.

<BufferAnatomy />

The view picks its own pace. Every 0.2 seconds it takes one value from the latest batch, scales it into the range 0 to 1, and appends it as a new bar on the right while the oldest bar slides off the left. That is the scrolling ribbon you see in a voice-message bubble: five bars per second, so a screen-wide ribbon shows the last ten seconds or so of the voice. Press the button and speak, if your browser allows the microphone; the demo runs entirely on your device and nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. Without a microphone it plays a simulated voice.

<VoiceWaves />

The level of a slice is its **RMS** (root mean square): square every sample, average the squares, take the square root. Squaring removes the sign, since a swing downward is as loud as a swing upward. Apple's Accelerate framework does this in one vectorised call, [`vDSP_rmsqv`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accelerate/1450655-vdsp_rmsqv), which is much faster than a hand-written Swift loop over raw floats.

```swift
let segmentSize = frameLength / numberOfSegments
guard segmentSize > 0 else { return }

for segmentIndex in 0..<numberOfSegments {
    let segment = bufferData.advanced(by: segmentIndex * segmentSize)
    var rms: Float = 0
    vDSP_rmsqv(segment, 1, &rms, vDSP_Length(segmentSize))

    let decibels = 20 * log10(rms)
    let amplitude = 1.1 * pow(10, decibels / 20)
    let bar = min(max(amplitude, 0), 1) * 10
    amplitudeValues.append(Double(bar))
}
```

A detail worth being honest about: converting to decibels and straight back with `pow(10, dB / 20)` cancels out mathematically, so the bar height is simply the slice's RMS scaled by 1.1 and clamped. The decibel step is a hook for a future non-linear scale; today it is a round trip. We kept it because decibels are the natural unit to reason about loudness in, and because it costs almost nothing. A real dB scale would also help: normal speech has an RMS of roughly 0.01 to 0.1, so on a linear scale most bars stay small. The built-in microphone's input gain usually cannot be changed from the app, so the scale is the only lever. Mapping a decibel range such as −60 to 0 dBFS (decibels relative to full scale) linearly onto the bar height puts normal speech, around −40 to −20 dBFS, in the middle of the ribbon instead of on the floor.

Two more honest notes. The guard matters, because a buffer shorter than 100 frames would give a slice size of zero, and the resulting NaN would flow straight into the UI. And with roughly 4,800 frames per callback, each slice covers about 1 ms, shorter than a single cycle of a low voice. A slice that short follows the shape of the wave more than its loudness, which is why the bars flicker; a 10 to 30 ms window would track how loud the voice actually sounds.

The bigger gap is in the view. It keeps one 1 ms value per 0.2 s tick, about half a percent of the audio, so a syllable that peaks between two ticks never shows. Each tick should take the loudest value (or the RMS) of everything that arrived since the last one, and the bar should rise fast and fall back over a few hundred milliseconds, the way a studio meter does.

> **In plain words:** to draw a level bar, take a tiny slice of the wave, measure how hard it is swinging on average, and draw that. The recorder does it a hundred times per buffer; the view picks one value five times a second and scrolls.

## Cutting a river into cups

We do not wait until the user stops talking and send one big recording. That would make the person sit through silence before seeing a single word. Instead the recorder works like a river filling cups: it writes into one small WAV file, and when the cup is full, closes it, hands it to the rest of the app, and opens the next one.

<ChunkTimeline />

How full is full? A timer, built on Combine's `Timer.publish`, closes the current file at a fixed interval. The interval can be given in seconds or in bytes, but bytes are not actually counted: the SDK converts them to a time using a fixed ratio, where the default of 100,000 bytes means a 0.8 second timer. That ratio, 125,000 bytes per second, matches no real format (48 kHz mono 16-bit is 96,000). So the real size of a chunk depends on the format the microphone delivers; at 48 kHz mono 16-bit it is about 77 KB, and after resampling to 16 kHz it shrinks to about 25 KB. And since a chunk can only close between whole tap buffers, its actual length varies by about one buffer, around 100 ms:

```swift
public enum AudioChunkSplitOption {
    case bySeconds(seconds: TimeInterval)
    case byData(amount: Int)
}
```

Each finished chunk is announced through a Combine publisher, `newAudioChunkPublisher`. The recorder knows nothing about networks. It records and publishes. Whoever subscribes decides what to do with the files.

Swapping cups is where the SDK leaks. To close a chunk it drops its reference to the file, which is what makes `AVAudioFile` write the final sizes into the header, publishes the URL on the main actor, and only then creates the next file on a background task. Until that file exists, the tap writes to `nil`, which silently does nothing, so any buffer that lands in the gap is lost. The timer has its own blind spot: it runs on the main run loop in the default mode, which pauses while a scroll view is being dragged, so a user who scrolls the chat while talking stops chunk rotation until they let go.

## Speaking the server's language

The microphone's native rate is whatever the hardware gives, commonly 44.1 or 48 kHz. The speech service wants 16 kHz, 16-bit, one channel. Between the two sits a resampling service with two strategies:

- [`AVAudioConverter`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudioconverter) for any ratio. It handles awkward conversions such as 44,100 to 16,000 with proper filtering, and it is the one the app uses.
- A [`vDSP_desamp`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/accelerate/1450349-vdsp_desamp) path for whole-number decimation ratios. `vDSP_desamp` filters and then keeps every Nth sample. Ours passes N taps of 1.0 each, a box that is barely an anti-aliasing filter: for 48 to 16 kHz, three taps are only 3.5 dB down at the new 8 kHz limit and 9.5 dB down at 12 kHz, where clean decimation wants around 60 dB. The taps are not divided by N either, so the level comes out three times too high and clips above a third of full scale. And the ratio is truncated to a whole number without a check: 44.1 to 16 kHz decimates by 2, comes out 27 percent slow and loses the last quarter of every chunk. It can only go down. It exists in the SDK but the app does not use it, so the choice is a proper filter or deleting it.

```swift
// simplified
let capacity = AVAudioFrameCount(Float(buffer.frameLength) * targetSampleRate / originalSampleRate)
guard let outputBuffer = AVAudioPCMBuffer(pcmFormat: outputFormat, frameCapacity: capacity),
      let converter = AVAudioConverter(from: buffer.format, to: outputFormat)
else { throw AudioResamplingError.resamplingFailed }

var error: NSError?
converter.convert(to: outputBuffer, error: &error) { _, outStatus in
    outStatus.pointee = .haveData // every time, with the same buffer
    return buffer
}
if error != nil { throw AudioResamplingError.resamplingFailed }
```

The input block is the classic trap with this API. The converter calls it whenever it wants more audio, and this one answers `.haveData` with the same buffer every time. Given room, the converter would take it at its word and feed the start of the chunk in again, duplicating it at the end of the output. What saves the SDK is the capacity: the output buffer holds exactly the chunk's length at the new rate, so the converter stops when it is full. It still asks for more input near the end, because its filter needs a few samples of look-ahead to finish the last outputs, and what it gets is the start of the same chunk. So the last fraction of a millisecond of every chunk is filtered against the wrong audio: a tiny seam, not a repeat.

The fix is to hand the buffer over once and then report `.endOfStream`, which tells the converter the chunk is complete and lets it finish the tail against silence. Two more details: the status `convert` returns is ignored today, and `.haveData` is the normal success status whether or not everything fit, so an output buffer that comes back exactly full (`frameLength == frameCapacity`) is the only sign something was cut off. Size it with a little room for the tail. A converter that lives for the whole stream works differently again (see the end).

<ResampleConverter />

Why not record at 16 kHz in the first place? Because in practice the input node's format is the hardware's to decide. `setPreferredSampleRate` is only a request the system is free to ignore, and a tap on the input node has to use the hardware's own format.

A Bluetooth headset can even run at a lower rate than the phone's own microphone. A narrowband hands-free link samples at 8 kHz, and resampling to 16 kHz cannot add back what was never captured: the service gets phone-line audio with nothing above 4 kHz, consonants included. Most current headsets negotiate 16 kHz wideband speech, but the input route (`currentRoute.inputs`) and the session's sample rate are worth logging, because together they explain a sudden drop in accuracy.

We take what the microphone offers and convert afterwards, which also means the temporary chunk files are not 16 kHz yet. That keeps recording robust across devices, headsets and Bluetooth routes, and puts the one risky transformation in a single, testable place.

## The 4,096-byte header

This is the bug we like telling most, because it is invisible if you only look at code and obvious the moment you look at bytes.

A WAV file is a short header followed by raw sound. The textbook header is 44 bytes that say "this is a WAV, it is mono, 16,000 samples per second, 16 bits each, and the sound is this many bytes long." After it come the samples.

Apple's file writer does not write the textbook version. `AVAudioFile`, which writes every chunk in the SDK, the recorded one and the resampled one, adds a `JUNK` chunk of 28 empty bytes, room for 64-bit sizes should the file ever pass 4 GB, and a `FLLR` chunk of filler that pushes the sound to exactly byte 4,096. Nothing in the Swift says so; a hex dump of one chunk does.

<WavByteMap />

Every chunk we record is its own complete WAV file, with its own header. That is fine for a file. But our chunks are not independent files: they are consecutive slices of one continuous stream. While each one went out with its own header, the audio reaching the speech service had audible clicks. Removing the headers made them go away, and the bytes show why. Read as 16-bit sound at 16 kHz, a 4,096-byte header is 2,048 samples, 128 ms. Most of it is filler, all zeros: an eighth of a second of silence spliced into every 0.8 s chunk. At either end of that silence sit the chunk names and sizes, and they make very loud samples: `fmt ` decodes to 28,006 out of a possible 32,767, `RIFF` to 18,770, `data` to about 24,900, against normal speech around 300 to 3,000. Every chunk boundary carried a burst 20 to 40 dB louder than the voice, a gap, and another burst. One caveat: each chunk is also resampled on its own, so the converter's filter starts fresh at every boundary. That leaves a far smaller seam than the header did, but it is a seam, and one more reason to stream instead of filing (see the end).

The fix was a small byte-level service that strips the header and sends only the sound. The format is not lost, because the connection handshake already declares it up front: 16,000 Hz, 2 bytes per sample, 1 channel.

The original intent was to keep the header on the very first chunk only. In practice the "is this the first chunk?" check runs after the chunk has already been added to the recorded list, so it never sees an empty list and every chunk, the first included, goes out header-free. Nothing breaks, because the handshake carries the format, but it is a good reminder to test what the code does, not what you meant it to do.

Stripping a header sounds like "drop the first 44 bytes". Here that cut would land inside the `JUNK` chunk and send 4 KB of header as sound. So the parser walks the file the way the format intends:

```swift
// simplified
guard wavData.count > 44 else { return nil }
guard wavData.prefix(4) == Data("RIFF".utf8), wavData[8..<12] == Data("WAVE".utf8) else { return nil }

var offset = 12
while offset + 8 < wavData.count {
    let chunkID = wavData.subdata(in: offset..<offset + 4)
    var sizeValue: UInt32 = 0
    _ = withUnsafeMutableBytes(of: &sizeValue) { wavData.subdata(in: offset + 4..<offset + 8).copyBytes(to: $0) }
    let chunkSize = Int(UInt32(littleEndian: sizeValue))

    if chunkID == Data("data".utf8) {
        let start = offset + 8
        return wavData.subdata(in: start..<min(start + chunkSize, wavData.count))
    }
    offset += 8 + chunkSize
}
return nil
```

Check the magic words, then hop from chunk to chunk, reading each one's four-letter name and its little-endian size, until you land on `data`. That walk is what makes the 4 KB header harmless: `JUNK` and `FLLR` are skipped by their sizes, with no fixed offset assumed. A header-only chunk comes back as empty data rather than a failure. Two RIFF rules it leaves out cost a line each: a chunk with an odd size is followed by a pad byte, which `offset += 8 + chunkSize` does not step over, and the data is not trimmed to an even byte count, so a truncated file could hand over half a sample and shift every 16-bit sample after it. Apple's writer produces neither case, which is why neither has bitten.

The size it trusts also has to be final. `AVAudioFile` writes 0 into the `data` size when it creates a file and fills in the real value only when the file is released. Read early, the parser finds a `data` chunk of size 0 and returns an empty chunk, without an error. The recorder releases the file before publishing it, so the normal path is safe; a tap task still writing at that moment is not (see the end).

If the parser fails, it returns nil and the original chunk is sent instead. That rule we would change: bytes that do not parse are not slightly noisy audio but, with this header, an eighth of a second of silence between two full-scale clicks, or garbage if the file is broken, and a burst of noise can make a recogniser invent words. Sending the same length of silence keeps the timing and costs nothing.

> **In plain words:** a sound file starts with a label describing itself. The server was told what the sound is when the conversation began, so the label is dead weight, and worse, it caused clicks and gaps.

## Over the wire

Now each chunk is 16 kHz, header removed, ready to travel. The transport is a WebSocket, a connection that stays open so audio can flow one way while text flows back the other, in real time.

The speech service's protocol is made of JSON text events, so the binary audio is wrapped as base64 (a way of writing any bytes as plain text, at about a third more size) inside a small JSON event. A WebSocket can carry binary frames too, and a protocol that accepted them would skip that overhead. The handshake that opens the conversation, and every message after it, follows a strict order:

<SttSequence />

Three design decisions in that diagram carry most of the weight:

- **The socket gates the microphone.** We do not start recording until the server has confirmed it is ready. There is no point capturing audio that has nowhere to go.
- **The handshake states the format.** The app's opening message declares 16,000 Hz, 2 bytes per sample, 1 channel. The server never has to guess what the bytes mean, which is exactly why the WAV header is not needed.
- **Text arrives in two flavours.** A **partial text** message carries text that is rewritten as the sentence grows, which is what makes words appear live. A **final text** message carries the complete sentence and ends the turn.

## One app, then one SDK

The whole thing started inside the chat app itself, as ordinary view-model code. As it grew, three things became clear:

- Audio capture, resampling and WAV handling are not chat features. They are a reusable capability.
- They needed their own tests and their own release cadence.
- The view model had started to know far too much about audio.

So the voice code moved into a dedicated Swift package. The app is now a client of it. The package exposes small protocol-based services that the app can compose or replace:

- **`AudioEngineRecordingService`** records, chunks and measures levels, and publishes chunks, amplitudes and duration as Combine streams.
- **`AudioResamplingService`** converts any recorded file to the sample rate a service expects.
- **`WAVProcessingService`** takes the header off a chunk.
- **`AudioSessionManager`** and **`AudioFileManager`** wrap the system session and file handling behind protocols, so the recorder can be tested without a microphone.

<SdkMap />

From the app's side, using it is a handful of subscriptions: one for new chunks, one for amplitudes. (A publisher here is just a stream of values you can subscribe to, from Apple's Combine framework.)

---

## What it added up to

A person speaks, and while they are still talking:

- Their voice is **sampled by the hardware**, tapped through one input node, and used two ways: written into a small file for the server, and reduced to level values for the screen.
- It is **cut into small files** continuously, so recognition starts while they are still talking.
- Each file is **resampled to 16 kHz**, stripped to raw sound, **base64 encoded**, and sent over an open WebSocket.
- Partial text streams back and rewrites itself; the final text closes the turn and the microphone shuts off in a fixed, safe order.

<PipelineJourney />

The result is a speech feature that the rest of the app treats as three Combine publishers and two method calls. All the sound is behind that line.

## What we would do differently

Honest engineering notes, in the order we would tackle them:

- **Stream, don't file.** Resample inside the tap with one long-lived converter and send raw 16 kHz PCM every few hundred milliseconds. The headers, the temporary files, the bytes-to-seconds timer and the per-chunk filter seams all disappear at once, and the audio is rounded to 16-bit once, after resampling, instead of once when the chunk is written and again after it is resampled. Two details make or break it: once the input block has handed over the current buffer it answers `.noDataNow`, which keeps the filter's state, and `.endOfStream` only when recording stops; and `AVAudioConverter` is not thread-safe, so the conversion has to run on one serial path, which is the next point.
- **Do not spawn a task per audio buffer.** Unstructured tasks do not run in order, so buffers can reach the file out of sequence or race the timer that rotates files. The tap is not the real-time render thread, so write from it directly, or hand buffers to one serial writer.
- **Swap files without a gap.** Create the next file before releasing the current one, and do the swap on the same serial path as the writes. Today the tap writes into `nil` while the next file is being created, so buffers in that window vanish, and the current file is read and replaced from several threads with no synchronization. A tap task that is mid-write when the chunk is published also keeps the file open, and an open file still says its sound is 0 bytes long.
- **Flush the last chunk before saying goodbye.** "End of audio" must only go out after the final, partial chunk has been closed, resampled and sent, or the last words of a sentence can be lost. `stopRecording` makes that hard: its work runs in a task nobody awaits, so it returns before the final chunk is even published, and any error inside is lost. It also opens a fresh file after the final save, leaving an empty temporary file behind every session.
- **Count real bytes, or just use time.** The bytes-to-seconds ratio hides the fact that chunk size depends on the hardware rate. A plain duration would be clearer, and counting frames in the tap would be exact. Either way the timer belongs in the common run-loop modes, so scrolling does not pause it.
- **Name the sample rate.** The chunk file settings give a channel count and a bit depth but no `AVSampleRateKey`. On macOS, `AVAudioFile` then labels the file 8,000 Hz and writes 48 kHz samples into it unconverted. Recognition works in production, so iOS evidently fills in the right rate, but nothing in the code says so, and one key removes the doubt.
- **Test the first-chunk logic.** A one-line unit test would have shown that the header check never fires.
- **Measure latency end to end.** We tuned chunk length by feel. A timestamp at the tap and another at the first partial text would turn that into a number.

---

## Takeaways

> **Look at the bytes.** The clicking bug lived in a 4,096-byte header that no amount of reading Swift would have revealed.

> **Let the recorder measure and the view pace.** Moving the timer out of the recorder made each side simpler; the next step is a 10 to 30 ms window, a dB scale, and a tick that keeps the loudest value since the last one.

> **Do not trust a node until it has a real format.** Check sample rate and channel count before installing the tap and before starting the engine, or crash.

> **Let the microphone choose its own rate, then convert**, and tell the server the format once, up front.

> **Test what the code does, not what you meant.** An "is this the first chunk?" check that can never be true is invisible until you trace it.
